Cover Letter

Dear Editor,

We submit our manuscript entitled "Allocation or Direction? A
Matched-Distortion Audit of Visual Location-Privacy Mechanisms" for
consideration as a Regular Paper in IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security.

Street-level imagery is routinely released after geographic metadata are
stripped, yet the pixels themselves remain matchable against geo-tagged
reference databases. Visual location-privacy mechanisms answer this by
spending a bounded distortion budget, and the field designs them almost
entirely by choosing how that budget is *allocated* across the image -- which
regions to protect, and how strongly. Our manuscript asks whether that design
choice is what actually buys privacy against a retrieval attacker.

The contribution is an evaluation result and the protocol that produces it,
not a new sanitizer. Our protocol holds *delivered* distortion fixed, solved
per frame through the pixel clamp, and varies one design axis at a time. This
matters because matching nominal noise scale rather than delivered distortion
leaves a clipping confound in place, and because a mechanism can produce a
plausible privacy-quality curve without performing any spatial selection at
all. We demonstrate the latter concretely: the learned support map of the
CRF-inspired mechanism we implement as a testbed is numerically constant.

Our principal findings on a real place-labelled Mapillary Street-Level
Sequences benchmark, read by five held-out attackers and by a gallery-free
image-to-GPS model, are:

- What an allocation rule buys depends on which attacker reads the frame. On
  four of the five, none of nine energy-matched placement rules, three
  published-segmentation-model placements or four perturbation operators beats
  spreading the budget uniformly; every difference lies within +/-0.012 Top-1.
  On the fifth, three rules beat uniform by 0.031 to 0.055 Top-1, among them
  the rule our own margin analysis derives, whose inverse is correspondingly
  worse. We state that null as a bounded non-detection rather than as an
  absence: equivalence at our declared margin is certified in three of nine
  non-degenerate cells and left undecided in the rest at the resolution 400
  query clusters afford, and we print the number of clusters certifying it
  would take.

- Direction behaves differently. A perturbation steered away from the released
  frame's own clean embedding under a surrogate ensemble -- requiring neither
  white-box access nor the attacker's gallery -- transfers to all five
  held-out attackers, moving Top-1 from 0.197 to 0.032 on the weak attacker
  and from 0.780 to 0.732 on the strong one, with place-clustered intervals
  excluding zero.

- The asymmetry survives a change of attacker class that our explanation of it
  does not. Against a model that emits a coordinate rather than ranking a
  gallery, the same direction cuts city-level localisation from 0.713 to 0.546
  while neither placement rule we tried moves it. Our mechanistic account is
  stated for a correct-versus-hardest-negative ranking margin, and such a
  model has no gallery and no rank to flip, so we report this as a fact about
  the finding and a limit on the explanation rather than as a confirmation.

- A decomposition identifies which part of the perturbation carries the
  effect. Shuffling its signs while preserving per-pixel magnitudes removes
  the effect entirely; flattening the magnitudes while preserving the signs
  retains 49-99% of it.

We have taken care to state these results at the strength the evidence
supports, and we draw the Editor's attention to the qualifications the
abstract itself carries. We claim no formal differential-privacy guarantee,
and say so explicitly. Against the strong retriever, Top-5 and Top-10 fall far
less than Top-1, so we describe the effect as ranking suppression rather than
as location privacy. Attacker-side preprocessing recovers much of the effect
unless the direction is optimised over those transforms. An attacker that
trains an inverse -- the most damaging of the four, and the one we run against
all four retrieval attackers rather than the two it was cheapest to run
against -- takes back between half and all of what the direction buys, by an
amount its architecture and not its accuracy decides. And at matched
distortion the direction spends more detector and segmenter accuracy than
isotropic noise, so that on our own admissibility analysis no budget we tested
is both admissible and effective. We report these costs rather than confining
them to a limitations paragraph, and the abstract says in its last sentence
that the contribution is the protocol and the measurement rather than a
deployable mechanism.

On the utility side we also give the tolerance a reference scale rather than
asserting one: the mIoU tolerance against which we judge admissibility is
calibrated against the spread between six published segmenters scored on the
same images, and we report both a generous and a strict tolerance so the Editor
can see which verdicts depend on that choice.

We also wish to be explicit about positioning. Mask-guided adversarial
perturbation has been proposed for location privacy before, and we cite and
discuss that work directly. Our novelty claim is not the perturbation but the
matched-distortion comparison between allocation and direction, and we
implement a mask-guided multi-model baseline under our own protocol: it never
beats the unmasked direction and degrades monotonically as the mask tightens,
across three mask sources.

The work fits the scope of TIFS as an evaluation and measurement contribution
to information forensics: it concerns the inference of private location
information from released visual data, and its practical recommendation is
that learned sanitization maps be reported against allocation-matched
controls. We believe the negative result is the useful part, and that
publishing it is preferable to leaving the field's central design assumption
untested.

One request about length, which we would rather put to you explicitly than
resolve by omission. The SPS guidance recommends that supplementary
material not exceed six double-column pages without Editor-in-Chief approval,
and ours is eleven. We are asking for that approval. The reason is specific to a
paper of this kind: the submission reports a negative result against an
assumption the field designs around, and the TIFS guidance for deep-learning
submissions holds such a paper to a higher standard of reproducibility than an
ordinary one. A referee testing a negative result needs to see the cells it was
pooled from, not a summary of them. Those extra pages are what let us bring
inside the submission the per-rule placement study the central null is pooled
from, the same nine rules extended to the three attackers that break the null,
the solved-allocation arm the manuscript's positive allocation result rests
on, the operator arm, the purification result across all four attackers, the
head-to-head against the closest published method, the two paired per-image
utility tables, the adaptive attacker's per-condition breakdown, a table
enumerating every placement rule with its provenance, and the geolocator arm
--- the one attacker class that does not rank a gallery at all. Each sits
beside the text that reads it, and each recomputes from the released per-query
rows without a GPU.

The main text is within the thirteen-page limit for an initial regular-paper
submission. Reaching it cost no result: the author biographies will be
supplied at acceptance, the pipeline diagram and the search-budget curve are
in the supplement, and the rest came from compression and from retiring
references that belonged to an earlier framing of this work.

This manuscript has not been published previously and is not under
consideration elsewhere. All authors have approved the submission. The code,
the benchmark manifests and the per-query exports behind every reported table
are deposited at IEEE DataPort (doi 10.21227/jnr0-jm15), cited in the manuscript,
together with the extended
evidence report the manuscript cites; no credentials are required, and the
table verification runs without a GPU.

Thank you for your time and consideration.
